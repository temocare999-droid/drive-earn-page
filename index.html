Check whether you'd actually qualify.

Most applications fail on one of these four things. Worth reading before you spend 15 minutes applying.

You, the driver

  • Meet the minimum driving age for your city — usually 21 for rides, sometimes 18–19 for delivery only
  • At least one year of licensed driving history (three years if you're under 23 in many markets)
  • A valid U.S. driver's license in your name
  • Legal authorization to work in the United States
  • A smartphone that can run the driver app

Your vehicle

  • Four doors and room for at least four passengers for rideshare
  • Model year limits vary by city — often within the last 10–15 years
  • No commercial branding, no salvage or rebuilt title
  • Passes a local vehicle inspection
  • Delivery-only is far more flexible — in many cities a bike, scooter, or older car works

Paperwork

  • Proof of personal auto insurance with you listed on the policy
  • Vehicle registration in your name or a documented relationship to the owner
  • Background and driving-record check — recent DUIs, reckless driving, or violent offenses are typically disqualifying
  • Some cities require a local permit, business license, or extra inspection

Money and tax setup

  • A bank account for weekly deposits
  • You're an independent contractor, not an employee — no withholding, no benefits
  • Expect a 1099 form and quarterly estimated taxes if you earn meaningfully
  • Track your miles from day one — the standard mileage deduction is usually the single biggest write-off

Questions worth answering before you apply.

Straight answers, including the parts that aren't flattering.

How long does approval actually take?

The application itself takes roughly 15 minutes. The wait is the background check, which typically runs three to seven business days but can stretch to two weeks in busy markets or if your record needs manual review. Vehicle inspection is scheduled separately and is often the real bottleneck — book it as soon as you're able to.

Is the sign-up bonus guaranteed?

No. Bonus offers change constantly, vary by city, and almost always require completing a set number of trips inside a time window — for example a certain number of rides in your first 30 days. Some markets have no bonus at all at a given moment. The exact offer available to you is shown inside the app during sign-up, and that number is the only one that counts. Treat any figure quoted elsewhere, including on this page, as illustrative.

What does it really cost me to drive?

Fuel is the obvious one, but it's rarely the biggest. Depreciation, tires, brakes, oil changes, and eventual major repairs add up to roughly $0.15–$0.25 per mile for most cars. On top of that you're responsible for your own taxes. A useful habit: subtract fuel, set aside 15–20% for taxes, and only then look at what's left as your actual hourly rate.

Do I need commercial insurance?

You need your own personal policy to apply. While you're actively on a trip, the platform provides its own coverage layer, but there are gaps — particularly during the period when you're online and waiting for a request. Many personal policies exclude rideshare use entirely, which can leave you exposed. Call your insurer before you start and ask specifically about rideshare or delivery endorsements.

Can I do this with a leased or borrowed car?

Leases often contain mileage caps and clauses restricting commercial use — read yours before you rack up miles. For a borrowed car, you generally need to be listed on the insurance policy and be able to document your relationship to the registered owner. Rental options exist through partner programs, but weekly rental costs eat a large share of earnings, so run the numbers carefully first.

What disqualifies someone?

The common ones are a DUI within the last seven years, reckless driving, driving with a suspended license, multiple recent moving violations, or any violent or sexual offense. Standards are stricter for rides than for delivery. Minor issues from many years ago are often fine. If you're unsure, applying is free and the check will tell you.

How and when do I get paid?

Standard payouts run weekly to your linked bank account. Most platforms also offer instant cash-out for a small per-transfer fee. Tips are yours in full. Remember that nothing is withheld for taxes — if you don't set money aside yourself, you'll owe it in a lump at filing time.

Is this a job with benefits?

No. You're an independent contractor. There's no health insurance, no paid time off, no unemployment coverage, no guaranteed minimum, and no manager to escalate to. The trade you're making is stability for schedule control. That trade works well for some people and badly for others, and it's worth being honest with yourself about which you are before you build a budget around it.

Which is better to start with — rides or delivery?

Delivery generally has lower barriers: looser vehicle rules, a faster path to approval, and no passengers in your car. Rides typically pay more per hour in most markets but demand a newer vehicle and a cleaner record. Plenty of drivers do both and switch based on time of day. If you're testing the waters, delivery is the cheaper experiment.
